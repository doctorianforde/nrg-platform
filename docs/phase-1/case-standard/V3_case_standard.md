**NRG CASE STUDY**

**QUESTION PROMPT FORMAT**

**Version 3 - RENR Clinical Judgment Case Study Writing Standard**

| **Purpose:** A standardized master specification for writing unfolding, entry-level nursing case study questions for RENR preparation. It combines a six-step clinical-judgment sequence with the RENR domains and taxonomy levels while retaining the four-option single-best-answer format. |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

| **Non-negotiable scope:** All knowledge, assessment, interventions, delegation, communication, professional judgment, and decision-making must remain within the expected scope of an entry-level registered nurse in the Caribbean context. Do not test specialist-only knowledge unless the necessary information is provided in the case. |
|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

**AUTHORING PRINCIPLE**

NCLEX-style clinical judgment provides the reasoning sequence. RENR provides the domain and cognitive taxonomy. Each finalized question must therefore have ONE clinical-judgment step, ONE primary RENR domain, and ONE final taxonomy level.

# 1. Global Authoring Rules

- Write for the entry-level registered nurse, not the specialist or advanced-practice nurse.

- Every case must be clinically realistic, internally consistent, and follow a continuous timeline.

- The final diagnosis must not be revealed in the title or opening scenario unless the educational objective requires a known diagnosis.

- For the core diagnostic cases, the opening information should reasonably support at least 2-3 plausible hypotheses before later data narrow the problem.

- Do not visually flag abnormal data. Use the same font, weight, and formatting for normal and abnormal findings.

- Reveal information progressively. A student may use all information revealed up to the current question but must never need information that appears later.

- Each question must be answerable independently from the information available at that point. A wrong answer to an earlier question must not make a later question impossible.

- A case must end at a clinically logical decision point such as stabilization, admission, transfer, escalation of care, surgery, referral, discharge, follow-up, or another clearly defined outcome.

- Use one-best-answer multiple-choice items unless another item type is specifically requested.

- Do not include trick wording, obscure trivia, or irrelevant complexity simply to make the item difficult.

Taxonomy percentages apply across the full question bank, not within every individual case.

# 3. Case Title

The case title should be symptom-based, complaint-based, event-based, or situation-based. It must not reveal the final diagnosis in diagnostic cases.

- Keep the title concise: usually 5-10 words.

- Use wording consistent with what the patient, relative, bystander, or health professional would reasonably report.

- Do not include the final disease name, syndrome, or definitive diagnostic label in the title.

- Do not use a previous medical diagnosis as a hidden giveaway that the same condition is now acute unless that history is intentionally relevant.

**Examples:**

**GOOD: Collapse During the Third Trimester** \| BAD: Eclampsia with HELLP Syndrome

**GOOD: Sudden Weakness During Breakfast** \| BAD: Acute Ischaemic Stroke

**GOOD: The Deteriorating Postoperative Patient** \| BAD: Sepsis Secondary to Anastomotic Leak

# 4. Case Presentation (Opening Scenario)

Introduce the patient and clinical setting without revealing the final diagnosis. The opening scenario should create a believable bedside story and provide enough information for the first question without overloading the learner.

- Use first name only, age, sex/gender as clinically relevant, and gravida/para/gestational age when applicable.

- State the care setting or source of admission: community, clinic, emergency department, ward, theatre recovery, mental health unit, etc.

- Include the event or story leading to the presenting complaint.

- Use the patient or relative's own words when appropriate.

- Identify the source of information: patient, relative, bystander, EMT/paramedic, or existing record.

- Laypersons should use lay language; health professionals may use appropriate clinical terminology.

- Include only medical, surgical, medication, allergy, social, family, obstetric, or psychiatric history that is relevant to the case or realistic context.

- Include relevant negatives when they are necessary to distinguish hypotheses; avoid listing negatives simply to give away the answer.

**Opening scenario template**

\[First name\], a \[age\]-year-old \[woman/man/person\] \[gravida/para and gestational age if applicable\], is brought to \[setting\] by \[source\] after \[chief complaint/event\]. \[Duration and associated symptoms\]. \[Relevant negatives if needed\]. \[Pertinent history/medications/allergies\].

# 5. Patient Data Presentation

## 5.1 Vital Signs

Present a clinically realistic set of vital signs for the relevant time point. Include normal/non-contributory values when appropriate so that the presence of a displayed number does not automatically signal that it is important.

- Use civilian timestamps consistently (e.g., 12:20 am, 3:00 pm).

- Standard observations usually include temperature, pulse/heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and SpO2.

- Add pain score, RBS/glucose (measured in mg/dL always), GCS, fetal heart rate, weight, urine output, or other measures only when clinically relevant.

- Do not label values high, low, critical, normal, abnormal, improving, or worsening. Let the student interpret them.

- When a later question occurs at a new time point, present updated observations rather than repeating the original set without purpose.

## 5.2 Physical Assessment

Document findings using objective, measurable language. Use a consistent body-systems order to help the learner organize data.

**Recommended order:** CNS -\> Respiratory -\> Cardiovascular -\> Gastrointestinal -\> Genitourinary/Obstetric -\> Integumentary -\> Musculoskeletal -\> Psychosocial/Comfort -\> Immunological/Infectious findings when relevant.

- Use objective language: e.g., "3+ bilateral pitting oedema" rather than "some swelling."

- Do not bold, colour, underline, or otherwise highlight abnormal findings.

- Do not label a body system normal/mild/moderate/severe; the student should determine severity.

- Do not repeat vital-sign values inside the physical assessment unless the repetition is clinically necessary.

- Do not interpret raw findings in the assessment section.

## 5.3 Laboratory and Diagnostic Reports

- Present laboratory values as raw results without high/low/critical flags.

- Organize by logical category: haematology, chemistry, urinalysis, microbiology, coagulation, etc.

- Reveal tests progressively. Do not place all future diagnostic information in the opening screen.

- Reference ranges may be included when educationally necessary, but do not use them to telegraph the answer.

- Radiology/ECG/ultrasound/CT/MRI or other diagnostic summaries should generally be concise and written as the reported finding or impression, not as an additional teaching paragraph.

- A diagnostic result introduced in a later question becomes available to all subsequent questions.

# 6. Progressive Disclosure and Timeline Control

The case must unfold in stages. The student may use all information revealed up to the current stage, but no answer may depend on data that are revealed later.

| **Stage**      | **Newly available information**                                                                                                                  |
|----------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Baseline       | Opening history + presenting complaint                                                                                                           |
| Q1             | Initial assessment and observations                                                                                                              |
| Q2             | Additional assessment or data needed to interpret the problem                                                                                    |
| Q3             | Additional labs/diagnostics or new findings that permit prioritization of the leading hypothesis                                                 |
| Q4             | Confirmed/working diagnosis or sufficient evidence to plan solutions; relevant orders may be introduced                                          |
| Q5             | Immediate action point: the nurse must implement, escalate, question, delegate, or perform the priority action                                   |
| Q6             | New response data after intervention: reassessment, vital signs, labs, symptoms, complications, or lack of response                              |
| Q7-Q8 optional | Domain-specific extension: communication, leadership, ethics, health promotion, professional development, care planning, discharge, or follow-up |

- Every intervention must be introduced before it is evaluated or referenced again.

- Every new time point should be clearly signposted as NEW INFORMATION.

- Do not refer to unstated events as though they already occurred.

- Use a logical clinical progression: assessment -\> interpretation -\> prioritization -\> planning -\> action -\> reassessment/outcome.

- Do not force every case to end in discharge. The endpoint must simply be clinically logical.

# 7. Core Six-Question Clinical Judgment Architecture

Questions 1-6 form the standard core. The clinical-judgment step is fixed by question position; apply one RENR domain and one taxonomy pre question classified from the actual construct tested (Section 2 rules), not forced by position.".

| **Question** | **Clinical Judgment Step** | **Primary purpose**                                                                                                                               |
|--------------|----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Q1           | Recognize Cues             | Identify significant/relevant information in history, observations, assessment, or records.                                                       |
| Q2           | Analyze Cues               | Interpret relationships among findings and determine what additional information is needed.                                                       |
| Q3           | Prioritize Hypotheses      | Determine the most likely or highest-priority problem/condition/complication supported by available evidence.                                     |
| Q4           | Generate Solutions         | Select an appropriate plan, intervention, anticipated order, preventive strategy, or expected outcome.                                            |
| Q5           | Take Action                | Choose the immediate nursing action: implement, hold/question, escalate, administer, communicate, or delegate as appropriate.                     |
| Q6           | Evaluate Outcomes          | Determine whether the patient improved, deteriorated, experienced a complication, failed to respond, or requires modification/escalation of care. |

## 7.1 Question 1 - Recognize Cues

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Recognize Cues

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Identify information that is relevant, abnormal, unexpected, safety-significant, or requires follow-up. Do not require the learner to establish the final diagnosis yet.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which finding requires the nurse's immediate follow-up?

- Which assessment finding is most concerning in this presentation?

- Which finding is most relevant to the patient's current problem?

**Authoring notes:**

- Do not visually identify the abnormal cue for the student.

- Avoid two findings of nearly equal priority unless the stem contains a clear priority principle.

- Q1 may be KC, AP, or ASE depending on how much interpretation or prioritization is required; do not automatically label it KC.

## 7.2 Question 2 - Analyze Cues / Determine Additional Data Needed

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Analyze Cues

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Interpret available findings and determine how they relate to one another. The item may ask for the most useful additional assessment, test, history, or finding needed to distinguish competing hypotheses.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which additional assessment is most important?

- Which laboratory investigation is most relevant?

- Which finding would best differentiate between the two leading hypotheses?

- Which information should the nurse obtain next?

**Authoring notes:**

- Test one construct per item. Do not combine assessment, diagnostic test, and treatment decisions in the same stem.

- The diagnosis should normally remain unrevealed at this stage.

- If asking about a diagnostic investigation, the task must still be appropriate for an entry-level nurse: recognize what is needed/anticipated, prepare for it, or question an inappropriate plan.

- Never ask the student to obtain data that were already explicitly supplied in an earlier stage. Before writing each question, compare the proposed answer against all previously revealed data. If already known, rewrite the item.

## 7.3 Question 3 - Prioritize Hypotheses / Identify the Priority Patient Problem

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Prioritize Hypotheses

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain, and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Use all data available to determine the most likely or most urgent problem. This may be a medical condition, nursing problem, complication, physiological problem, or explanation for the findings, but the stem must select only ONE of these constructs.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which condition is most consistent with the patient's findings?

- Which patient problem should the nurse prioritize?

- Which complication is the nurse most concerned about?

- Which explanation best accounts for the current assessment data?

**Authoring notes:**

- Do not write a stem such as "NANDA/medical/surgical/psychiatric diagnosis". Choose one construct.

- Diagnosis/condition distractors must be plausible and supported by some overlapping cues.

- The same diagnosis MAY be used in other cases if the, patient population, cue pattern, complication, or cognitive task is different. Do not duplicate substantially identical cases.

- Q3 will frequently be ASE because it requires integration and prioritization, but the final taxonomy must be assigned from the actual item.

## 7.4 Question 4 - Generate Solutions

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Generate Solutions

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Identify an appropriate plan before implementation. The learner determines what should be included in the plan, anticipated, prevented, or targeted as an outcome.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which intervention should be included in the plan of care?

- Which prescription should the nurse anticipate?

- Which outcome is appropriate for this patient?

- Which strategy would best reduce the identified risk?

**Authoring notes:**

- Do not turn Q4 into an immediate-action question. Planning and action are related but distinct.

- If the diagnosis is revealed here, do so through new clinical information rather than by simply announcing it without support.

- Do not automatically assign NP/CDM/NLM/HPMW as a bundle; classify the actual construct being tested.

## 7.5 Question 5 - Take Action

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Take Action

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain, and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Select what the nurse should do now, after the problem and plan are established. This is the implementation step.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which action should the nurse take first?

- Which prescribed intervention should the nurse implement now?

- Which order should the nurse hold or clarify?

- Which change requires immediate escalation?

- Which task may the RN appropriately delegate?

**Authoring notes:**

- Do not ask whether prior treatment worked; that is Q6.

- The primary domain may change based on the task: e.g., NLM for delegation, COM for urgent handover, PC for scope/accountability, NP or CDM for direct patient care.

- Use unsafe options only when safety is genuinely part of the construct.

## 7.6 Question 6 - Evaluate Outcomes

**Clinical Judgment Step:** Evaluate Outcomes

**RENR classification:** Assign ONE primary domain and ONE final taxonomy (KC, AP, or ASE) after the item is written.

**Focus:** Use new reassessment data to judge effectiveness, deterioration, complication, or need to modify/escalate care.

**Acceptable stem directions include:**

- Which finding best indicates that the intervention was effective?

- Which finding indicates deterioration?

- Which finding requires modification of the plan of care?

- Which finding suggests a treatment complication or adverse effect?

**Authoring notes:**

- Introduce new data after treatment or management has begun.

- Do not merely repeat the same assessment from Q1.

- Evaluation should connect directly to the intervention or expected outcome being assessed.

- Q6 will often be ASE, but assign taxonomy from the final item rather than by position alone.

# 9. Answer Option Construction

Each standard item contains four options (A-D) with one clearly best answer. The options must be parallel in structure and test the same construct.

- DISTRACTOR PLAUSIBILITY (must always apply): Every distractor must be something a competent entry-level nurse could genuinely consider in this situation. Reject any option that is obviously wrong, absurd, or unrelated to the case's clinical reality. Wrong answers must fail because of a subtle reasoning error (wrong priority, wrong construct, wrong timing, unsafe shortcut), never because they are silly.

<!-- -->

- The correct answer must be defensible from information available at that question stage.

- Distractors must be plausible to an entry-level learner and should reflect realistic errors in reasoning.

- Do not force a fixed distractor type into every question. Select distractors appropriate to the construct.

- Possible distractor mechanisms include: close/near-correct option, lower-priority action, sequencing error, common misconception, incomplete action, competing diagnosis, inappropriate action, or unsafe action.

- Use an unsafe distractor only when safety is genuinely being tested.

- Avoid obviously absurd options, joke answers, or unrelated diseases simply to create four choices.

- Avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above" unless specifically justified.

- Avoid grammatical clues, absolute wording, unequal option length, or repeated phrases that reveal the key.

- Do not repeat the same intervention in different wording across multiple options.

- Do not require compound options unless the item intentionally tests a combined action. Prefer one idea per option.

**Recommended internal distractor labels for rationale/analytics only:**

- Correct answer

- Close distractor

- Priority/sequencing distractor

- Incorrect/inappropriate distractor

- Unsafe distractor (only when applicable)

| **Student-view rule:** Do not show distractor labels, colours, domain, taxonomy, or rationale before the learner completes case study. |

# 10. One-Best-Answer Quality Test

Before accepting any MCQ, run all four checks. If any check fails, rewrite the item.

| **Check**                 | **Question to ask**                                                                                                         |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| A. Cover test             | Can a knowledgeable candidate formulate the answer before seeing the options?                                               |
| B. Two-answer test        | Could a competent nurse reasonably defend two options as equally correct? If yes, revise.                                   |
| C. Cue sufficiency test   | Is there enough information in the case at this stage to establish the correct answer?                                      |
| D. Hidden-assumption test | Does the keyed answer depend on unstated information, a local custom that was not specified, or information revealed later? |

### Case Evidence and Assumption Rule (**non-negotiable rule**)

**All questions must be answerable using only:**

1.  information explicitly provided in the case up to that question; and

2.  standard entry-level nursing knowledge required to interpret that information.

The learner must never be required to assume undocumented patient-specific facts.

Do not make an answer depend on an allergy, pregnancy status, laboratory abnormality, medication interaction, comorbidity, recent procedure, contraindication, diagnosis, or assessment finding unless that information has already been explicitly provided in the case.

If a patient-specific fact is necessary to make one option correct, reveal that fact before asking the question.

**The student should interpret the case—not complete the case with assumptions**

## Specifically for medications

### Medication Safety Question Rule

When a medication order is being questioned, held, clarified, or withheld, the case must provide the specific clinical reason that makes the order potentially inappropriate.

Examples include:

- documented allergy;

- contraindicating diagnosis;

- abnormal laboratory value;

- concerning vital sign;

- relevant drug interaction;

- pregnancy status;

- renal/hepatic impairment;

- current medication;

- previous adverse reaction.

Do not use a medication as the keyed "order to clarify" merely because a contraindication **could possibly exist**.

# 11. Answer and Rationale Output Format

After the learner answers, present the key and rationale in the following sequence. Colour/highlighting may be used in the rationale view only; text labels must also be included so colour is not the only cue.

1.  Answer: MCQ letter + full written answer.

2.  Correct-answer rationale: begin with "CORRECT:" and explain the clinical reasoning using the specific case data.

3.  Distractor rationales: explain why each option is less appropriate or incorrect, using priority, pathophysiology, safety, scope, sequencing, or context as relevant. State when a distractor WOULD be appropriate in another context if useful.

4.  Topic and subtopics.

5.  RENR primary domain + exact number/wording.

6.  RENR taxonomy: ONE of KC, AP, or ASE.

7.  Clinical Judgment Step.

8.  Suggested reading/review and prerequisite topics.

9.  Related links/tags for topic, subtopic, domain, taxonomy, or case series.

**Colour guide for rationale view only:** Correct = green; Close distractor = yellow; Priority/sequencing = orange; Incorrect/unsafe = red. Always include the text label in addition to colour.

# 12. Rationale Writing Standard

- Explain why the correct answer is best, not merely why it is true.

- Reference the patient-specific findings that support the decision.

- Connect the rationale to pathophysiology, nursing process, safety, evidence, scope of practice, or priority framework as appropriate.

- Explain why every distractor is not the best answer in this exact case.

- Do not invent data that were not presented.

- Do not use circular reasoning such as "this is correct because it is the priority." State why it is the priority.

- If local policy or protocol determines the answer, identify that dependency and ensure the protocol is specified or validated before publication.

# 13. Nursing Diagnosis / Care-Plan Questions

- When the item tests a nursing diagnosis, use accepted nursing-diagnosis structure and ensure the problem is supported by the case data.

- Actual diagnoses should use Problem + related to Etiology + as evidenced by defining characteristics when applicable.

- Risk diagnoses should use Problem + related to risk factor/etiology only; do not use "as evidenced by."

- Do not use a medical diagnosis as the etiology if the educational standard requires a pathophysiological or modifiable cause.

- When asking for priority nursing diagnosis, provide sufficient assessment data to distinguish competing diagnoses.

# 14. Clinical Validation Rule

| **Mandatory clinical review:** Every generated answer, medication dose, route, infusion rate, diagnostic interpretation, emergency intervention, pregnancy recommendation, paediatric recommendation, isolation precaution, anticoagulation plan, insulin instruction, resuscitation step, and safety claim must be independently clinically validated before publication or high-stakes use. |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

When exact doses or protocols vary by institution or country, either use the locally validated protocol or phrase the item so the reasoning is tested without relying on an unverified dose.

# 15. Pathophysiology Teaching Section

At the end of each completed case, include a teaching review that explains the anatomy, physiology, and pathophysiology behind the major findings and decisions.

- Normal anatomy and physiology of the affected system(s).

- Pathophysiological mechanism of the condition.

- How the presenting symptoms and assessment findings arise from the mechanism.

- How vital signs, laboratory values, and diagnostic findings connect to the pathophysiology.

- How the pathophysiology guided nursing priorities and interventions at each decision point.

- Relevant pharmacology and why the selected treatment works, when applicable.
